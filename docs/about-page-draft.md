## About page — full restructure

### 1. Opening (replaces "Personal memory and shared heritage" section)

**Heading:** What do we remember?

> Memory Library started with a question I kept coming back to: what gets remembered, and what gets left out?
>
> Museums and archives are good at holding onto certain kinds of history — the big events, the objects, the dates. Personal memory doesn't always work that way. Someone might remember a place through a shop that's gone, the smell of somebody's house, a song from a night out, a photograph kept in a drawer, the first computer they ever used. Those things rarely make it into an archive, but they can hold a huge amount of somebody's relationship with a place.

### 2. The idea

> When you put a lot of personal memories next to each other, they start to tell another version of a place, one that runs alongside the official history rather than replacing it. Memory Library is interested in finding different ways to record, share and experience memories like these, through art and, sometimes, technology.

### 3. Technology is secondary

> I work with technology, but Memory Library was never about the technology itself. The question for me is whether it can help someone notice, hold onto or share something that would otherwise be hard to keep. It's only by asking the right questions that people find the memory they weren't expecting, the detail that surprises even them, rather than the version of an event they think they're supposed to remember.

### 4. How it developed

> Memory Library began as a question, not a plan. I'd been making work about memory and sensory experience since 2018, and in 2019 I was back in Jersey directing a small team of sensory artists, working out how smell, taste, touch and personal recollection could be part of an artwork rather than something added afterwards.
>
> In 2020 I put a name to what I was actually trying to do: an immersive archive, a Memory Library, built from the experiences of people who had lived through the Nazi occupation of Jersey. The research collected audio, drawings, photographs, movement, even photogrammetry scans, but the question underneath all of it was simpler: what if you started with what somebody actually remembers, rather than what a museum decides is significant? Some people remembered Liberation Day through the hard-boiled sweets thrown into the crowd. For everyone else, the 9th of May doesn't taste like sweet orange and blackcurrant. Someone might tell you they remember standing in that same crowd aged five, and what's actually stayed with them is the smell of their mother.
>
> From there the idea grew past what I could make on my own. Resonate became a way for other artists to ask similar questions through their own work. Play Office, founded in 2024 with the Sustainable Conservation Trust, gave artists a space and the equipment to experiment, and was explicitly built as a pilot toward Memory Library. Past Makes Future added a different piece again, bringing people together to talk, compare notes, and connect Portsmouth with practitioners elsewhere. Somewhere in amongst all of that, the exchange became international, with artists and experiences from Bangladesh, Cairo, Lebanon, Malta and Jersey now part of the same conversation.
>
> The 2026 programme at Boathouse 5 brings several strands of that development together in one public programme. It's a significant step, not a finish line.

### 7. International exchange

> One of the questions I keep coming back to is what happens when a very local memory sits next to one from somewhere completely different. Portsmouth, Bangladesh, Cairo, Lebanon, Malta and Jersey are different places with different histories, and the details of a memory are rarely the same, but the feeling behind it can be surprisingly recognisable. That's part of why Memory Library works internationally as well as locally.
